Self-massage and balance training for NSEA swimmers. Rolling before practice helps you move more freely, and rolling after helps you feel less sore. The balance work builds the core and body control that keep you long and tight in a streamline. Pick an area on the body map, set the timer, and check off each exercise as you go.

Before you start
  • Roll muscle, not bone or joints. Skip the lower back, neck, kneecap, back of the knee, and the bony point of the hip.
  • Some discomfort is fine. Sharp pain, numbness, or tingling means stop and move the roller. Don't roll a fresh injury, bruise, or swollen area.
  • Balance work: go barefoot if you can, stay near a wall on a non-slip floor, and master each level on the floor first. Standing on full-round rollers is coach-supervised only.
  • This is for healthy swimmers. If something hurts during swimming, talk to your coach and an athletic trainer or physical therapist.

How to roll

Time per area
30–90 sec

Shorter before practice, longer after.

Pressure
5–7 out of 10

Firm but you can still breathe normally. Use a hand or foot to take weight off.

Speed
Slow

About an inch per second. Pause on tight spots and breathe.

When
Before & after

Before: a quick pass, then your normal warm-up. After: slower, as part of the cool-down.

Updated cueThe original handout said to hold each tender spot until the discomfort drops by 50–75%, however long that takes. Current practice uses a time limit instead: 30–90 seconds per area. Rolling longer doesn't add much, and grinding on one spot can leave it more sore.

Balance training

Sets
2–3 × 20–30 sec

Or 8–10 slow reps for squats, reaches, and push-ups.

Progression
Floor → flat → round

Floor first, then half roller flat side down, then flat side up, then full round.

Posture
Core tight

Keep your joints lined up. When you bend, use your hips, knees, and ankles together.

Move up when
It's easy

Tap your level on each card. Only move up when the current one is steady.

Pick an area

Tap a highlighted area on the front or back of the body to find the exercise that rolls it. Rolling works best on the big muscles swimmers use most: the lats and upper back for the pull, and the hips, quads, and calves for kicks and walls.

Figure numbers on the balance cards match that handout. Three cues have been updated to current practice (marked Updated cue).

Why rolling works. The handout says rolling relaxes muscle through the Golgi tendon organ. Current research points more to the nervous system: rolling lowers how much tension and soreness you feel and gives a short-term gain in range of motion, without hurting performance. It doesn't physically stretch or "break up" fascia. See Behm & Wilke (2019), Sports Medicine; Wiewelhove et al. (2019), Frontiers in Physiology; Chaudhry et al. (2008), Journal of the American Osteopathic Association.
