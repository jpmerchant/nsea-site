How long does a technique change take?

Think of a swimmer's current technique as a tank of hot water. Every deliberate rep of the new technique pours in cool water, and the overflow carries mixed water away. Old-way reps keep dripping hot water back in. Move the settings to see how fast the tank cools, in practice and under race stress.

How the model works

The tank follows the standard physics of a well-mixed tank with water flowing in and overflowing out. The gap between the tank and the incoming water closes exponentially: fast at first, slower as it gets close.

Tank size is anchored on Richard Schmidt's estimate, cited by Brent Rushall: about 300 trials make a habit and about 3,000 trials with meaningful feedback are needed to correct it. A 3-year habit is set at 3,000 reps for wall or length skills and 24,000 stroke cycles for stroke skills (Rod Havriluk: tens of thousands of repetitions). It grows with the square root of years, because older swimmers resist change more.

Deliberate focus is the share of focused reps done with full attention and feedback. Most of that feedback is the swimmer's own: what they feel, see and count. A coach, teammates, video or devices add to it. A focused rep without it counts about a fifth, as do unfocused reps done the new way. Rushall and Havriluk both find that reps without feedback rarely change technique.

Old-way reps practice the old habit. Each counts as a share of a deliberate rep (the weight setting). Leak and quality improve as the change takes hold; turn on Pure physics to hold them fixed.

Race stress: a learned pattern is never erased, only suppressed, and it can return under fatigue or pressure. The dashed line lags the practice line: it is the practice share squared, and catches up only as the change becomes very strong.

Practice design comes from the constraints-led approach and attention research. Task design (stroke-count or tempo targets, equipment, breathing patterns) makes reps cleaner and lets unfocused reps count up to twice as much (0.4 instead of 0.2). Race-pace share and cue style narrow the gap between practice and race stress: at 100% race pace with a simple image the two lines nearly meet. The sizes of these effects are illustrative choices, not measured values.

The numbers are illustrative. They show the shape of change and which settings matter most, not a promise for any one swimmer.
